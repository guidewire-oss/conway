The Flow Game · Player Briefing

You are about to run a platform org for a year.

Not your team — the whole thing. Every pod, every dependency, every tired on-call rotation. Your job is to make it flow. This page tells you how to play. It does not tell you what to do — that's the fun.

1The setup

You and your teammates take the controls of a real platform organization, frozen as it actually is today: its teams (we call them pods), spread across several cities and time zones, each carrying real work, real backlogs, and real people. From there, the future is yours to steer.

Time moves in quarters. Each quarter you make a small number of leadership moves, then hit Resolve and watch three months play out — work ships, queues build or drain, people thrive or tire, surprises arrive. Then you read what happened, learn from it, and decide the next quarter. After your final quarter, the org keeps running on its own for another year — living with whatever you left behind.

What you're trying to do

Get the most value out of the whole organization for the least total cost — while keeping promises, delighting the people who depend on you (inside and outside the company), and keeping your teams healthy enough to do it again next year. There is no single dial to crank. A quarter that ships a lot but burns out a team, or breaks a promise, or quietly stores up trouble for later, is not a good quarter.

2Reading the world

You don't need to be a metrics expert. You just need to read a few signals the way a doctor reads vitals — roughly, at a glance, knowing what "off" looks like. Here's the whole vocabulary:

Pod
A team. It has a size, a home city, and work on its plate.
WIP
Work in progress — how many things a pod has open at once. More isn't more done.
Queue load
How full a pod's plate is relative to what it can comfortably carry. Think of a motorway: it stays fast until it gets full, then a little more traffic makes everything crawl. Watch for pods running hot.
Cycle time
How long a piece of work takes to get done. Long cycle times usually mean a full plate, not lazy people.
Morale
How the people are holding up. It is slow to earn and quick to lose, and tired teams get slower and make more mistakes.
Upkeep & firefighting
The standing "tax" every pod pays to keep existing things running and to answer interruptions. It comes off the top, before any new work gets done.
Readiness
How prepared shipped work is for real life — monitoring, hand-off, the unglamorous parts. Low readiness feels free at launch and expensive later.
Dependencies
Who waits on whom. Some pods sit upstream of many others. Some hand-offs cross time zones where the working days barely overlap — those are the slow, costly seams.

That's it. If you can spot "this pod is overloaded," "these two are tired," "this hand-off is painful," and "we keep shipping things that aren't ready," you can play well.

3How a quarter works

  • Look. Read the board. Where is it red? Who's drowning, who's tired, what's overdue, which hand-offs hurt?
  • Choose. You have a tight budget of actions each quarter — only a few. You cannot do everything; that's the whole point. Pick the moves you believe will help most, given everything else.
  • Resolve. The quarter plays out. You'll get a short written story of what happened and why — treat it as your most valuable feedback. A running decision log remembers every quarter's choices and consequences.
  • Adjust. Use what the story told you to plan the next quarter. Between quarters, the world will also throw something at you — the kind of thing that really happens to platform teams serving big customers.

4Your levers

These are the moves available to you. Each one is described by what it's for. Each also has fine print — read it twice. Every lever buys you something and quietly asks for something back. The skill is not in knowing which levers are "good"; they are all good somewhere and wasteful elsewhere. The skill is in which ones you choose, on which pods, in which order, alongside which others.

Freeze work — pause some of a swamped pod's open work to relieve the queue. Relief you don't anchor has a way of drifting back.
Cap work-in-progress — limit how much a pod takes on at once. A limit set without easing what's already there can starve as surely as it soothes.
Hygiene sprint — spend a pod's effort cleaning up its tracking, sizing and quality. A quarter spent tidying is a quarter not spent shipping — which is sometimes exactly right.
Invest in an interface — turn a recurring hand-off between pods into self-service. It takes before it gives. Whether it ever gives depends on what you do around it.
Change interrupt handling — alter how a pod absorbs firefighting and interruptions. Freeing a team's hands can move the fire rather than put it out.
Reassign scope — move some work — and its ongoing upkeep — from one pod to another. Work travels faster than the knowledge to do it, and upkeep never travels alone.
Ship an MVP — deliver a smaller version, sooner. What you choose to leave out has its own opinion about when it comes back.
Require full readiness before starting — insist work is fully prepared before a pod begins it. Discipline slows the first step to steady all the others. The bill and the benefit arrive on different days.
Place a backfill — add one new person to a pod of your choice. People arrive sooner than they ramp; where you place them matters more than that you placed them.
Make an innovation bet — invest a pod's effort in something that pays off later. The fastest-looking wins and the longest-lasting ones rarely resemble each other at the start.
Commit a date — make a promise about when something lands. A promise kept compounds quietly; a promise missed echoes louder and longer than you'd expect.
A quiet word on the art of it: moves interact. Some pairs pull in the same direction and become more than their sum; others pull against each other and you pay for both while gaining little. Change is itself a load — pile on too much at once and the org strains under the churn alone, no matter how sensible each piece looked. The leaders who do best are rarely the ones who pull the most levers. They're the ones who feel the difference between a move that compounds and a move that merely shuffles — and who let one change settle before reaching for the next.

5How you're judged

At the end of every quarter, and at the very end, your stewardship is read across several dimensions at once. You're never told a formula, and no single dimension wins the game. Doing obviously well on one by quietly wrecking another is how good-looking quarters turn into bad years.

ReturnValue delivered against the total cost of delivering it.
TrustDid you keep the promises you made, on time?
DelightDid the people who depend on you — inside and out — get something solid?
Team healthAre your people sustainable, or running on fumes?
Holistic progressDid you move the whole system forward, or just patch corners?
The year afterThe org runs on its own once you stop. What you leave behind is part of your score.

That last one matters more than it looks. Short-term cleverness that leaves a fragile org behind does not survive the year that follows.

6The spirit of it

This is a flight simulator for running an organization, built on ideas you've been reading about. It is for learning, not testing. You will make trade-offs every single quarter — that's not a flaw in the game, it's the job. When a quarter goes sideways, read it as a signal, not a verdict. The teams that learn fastest, not the ones who guess a magic opening, tend to finish strongest.

Three habits travel well, in the game and at your desk:

  • Look before you act — find the one thing most holding the system back this quarter.
  • Read the story — the game explains cause and effect after every quarter; let it teach you.
  • Mind the people — flow and happy teams are the same problem, not competing ones.
