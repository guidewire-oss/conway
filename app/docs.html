What Conway does, and why

Most software planning tools answer "who is doing what?" Conway answers a harder question: "what will actually finish, by when, given the teams you have?"

It does this by reading your real delivery data from Jira — how long work actually takes at each team, which teams block which, how much work is already in flight — and using that history to forecast what a new plan can realistically deliver. It doesn't ask you to guess durations or trust optimism. It uses what already happened.

If you plan without respecting capacity, the busy teams become bottlenecks. Work queues behind them, dates slip, and the slip is invisible until it's too late to react. Conway makes the queue visible before you commit, so you can sequence work, freeze what won't finish, and add capacity only where it actually shortens the schedule.

You don't need to know any theory to use Conway. But if you're curious: the thinking comes from constraint management — every system has one bottleneck at a time, and improving anything except the bottleneck improves nothing. Conway finds that bottleneck (called the drum), protects it, and sequences everything else around it.

Start here — your first 15 minutes

New to Conway, or to the ideas behind it? Do this once on the demo plan. Every step below tells you what you will see — nothing can break.

  1. Open a plan. Plan ▸ Load demo plan. You land on the Network view: circles are pods (teams), arrows are real dependencies mined from work in flight, and the red rings are the pods closest to drowning. Don't study it yet — it is the "before" picture.
  2. Open the Order view (the tabs above). This is the heart of Conway. Every row is one initiative; the columns answer four questions: in what order, when can I promise it, what stops it starting sooner, and will the date hold.
  3. Read the banner. The coloured bar at the top is the whole plan in one sentence — "19 of 27 dates at risk" is a plan that will slip, with the worst offender named. Everything else on the page exists to explain that sentence.
  4. Open the Timeline (the tabs above). The same schedule drawn as bars over weeks. Find the red-ringed pod from step 1: its row is solid — it never idles. That is what a constraint looks like.
  5. Grab a bar and drag it. A week sideways moves its start; drop it and the engine re-plans everything in about a second. Watch the Order view behind you change. This is the point of Conway: every "what if" is answered by the engine, not by optimism.
  6. Press ⌘Z / Ctrl+Z. The last drag undoes. Mistakes are cheap.
  7. Open the Report (⎙ beside the tabs). One printable card: what will not finish, what is the constraint, what the engine would change and what it would cost. If you read nothing else before a planning meeting, read this.
  8. Freeze it. Click the chip that says baseline: none — save one ▸, name the order, save. You have just created the agreement you will measure the next three months against.

Nothing you do in steps 5–7 writes to Jira or to anyone's world outside Conway. The demo plan is synthetic. Break it freely.

The words, in plain language

Conway uses few words, but they are load-bearing. These are all of them — in the order you will meet them.

WordWhat it means here
poda team that owns work: a small group with its own queue and its own delivery record. Not an org-chart department.
trackone parallel line of work a pod can run. Capacity is tracks, not headcount — five developers who pair are ~three tracks, not five people's worth.
ρ (rho), utilizationdemand ÷ capacity. ρ 0.8 means the pod has room; ρ 1.0 means it is exactly full; ρ 1.2 means work arrives faster than it can leave, so a queue grows and everything waits longer than you think.
capacity lossthe slice of a track that never becomes product work: PTO, interviews, support, on-call, ramp. 10% means a week of capacity delivers 4.5 days of work. Every pod can override the plan's global figure.
WIPwork in progress — things started but not finished. High WIP is multitasking, and multitasking is how a busy team delivers less. Capping it is one of the cheapest levers.
drumthe busiest constraint pod. Its pace sets the pace of everything downstream — like a drumbeat. Protect it first.
binding constraintthe named reason a piece of work starts when it starts: a dependency, pod capacity, a WIP limit, a date lock, a freeze. Every start in the Order has one. No number without a reason.
verdictthe scheduler's honest answer per initiative: on-time, late (by how much), beyond-horizon (cannot start inside the period), structurally-infeasible (the date is impossible however work is ordered), no-date, or provisional (rests on unestimated work).
bufferthe lighter tail on a bar: the safety the plan carries. Dates you promise are bar-end plus buffer — protect the buffer, keep the promise.
baselinea frozen, named copy of the order and the inputs that produced it — "what we agreed". Not a backup: it is the measuring stick for every later change.
dispatch rulethe ordering strategy the scheduler used (critical-path-first, cheapest-cost-of-delay, stated priority…). ⚡ Optimize races all of them and shows the best.
effort vs wall-clocktwo estimate models. Effort: the estimate is total work, split across a pod's tracks (10 weeks on 2 tracks ≈ 5 calendar weeks). Wall-clock: the estimate already is calendar time. Pick with intent — the Order view explains both.
site overlaphow many working hours two sites share per day, from their real IANA timezones on the modelled date. A handoff across zero shared hours costs more than one across eight — and Conway prices the difference.
fever charta date-at-risk picture: buffer consumed vs chain progress. Green is comfortable, amber is burning fast, red has spent the buffer. Dots in red need a decision now.
full kitstarting work only when everything it needs is present. Starting early without the kit is not progress — it is WIP with worse dates.

Flow Actions

This is the triage screen. Three panels tell you, right now, what needs attention — and what you should stop doing.

The constraint

The busiest team, ranked by a queue score. The team at #1 is where flow chokes first. Watch whether the #1 card moves week to week — if it never changes, the org isn't acting on the bottleneck.

Bad multitasking — freeze candidates

Teams doing too many things at once. Each bar shows how far a pod's work exceeds a healthy WIP cap (the slider adjusts what "healthy" means). Red bars are the triage list: finish, freeze (out of sight, out of attention), or kill. Work that stays on the board keeps stealing attention even when nobody is working on it.

Buffer fever chart

In-flight epics as dots on a chart. Each dot's position shows whether the epic is consuming its safety margin faster than it's making progress. Green: say nothing. Yellow: plan a response. Red: the buffer is gone — renegotiate the date now, not at the end.

If you only read one card per week, read the #1 constraint card. Improving a non-bottleneck team improves nothing — the queue at the constraint sets the pace for everyone.

Org Network

The dependency map: which teams block which, drawn from real blocking history (not opinion). Left is upstream (platform teams), right is downstream (consumer-facing teams).

How to read it

  • Node size = how much work is in flight at that pod.
  • Colour = queue heat — how close the pod is to drowning.
  • Edge width = how many times that blocking link fired in the last 12 months. Arrow points at the blocked pod.

Feature Simulator

Answer "can we do this by then?" for one epic — before you commit. It runs 10,000 Monte Carlo trials over the task graph using each pod's real cycle-time distribution, so the forecast reflects reality, not optimism.

What to do

  1. Type a Jira epic key and press Import epic — tasks, pods and blocking links fill in from real data.
  2. Adjust task sizes or dependencies to match what you know.
  3. Press Run 10,000 trials and read the forecast.

Planning: the loop

Planning in Conway is a cycle, not a one-time act. You set up a plan, review the order, shape it, freeze what you accept, and re-plan when reality changes.

  1. Set up — attach a roster (a frozen copy of team composition as of today), upload the initiatives matrix, set the period start, horizon and capacity loss. The Plan setup disclosure holds all of this.
  2. Choose your models — WIP model (strict / drum-gated / off), estimate model (effort ÷ lanes vs wall-clock), optional splitting. The Set up this plan card offers recommended defaults with one click.
  3. Review the order — the Order view sequences everything by capacity. Your stated priority order is the working plan; the engine's best guess appears as a suggestion (↳N) you can accept or ignore.
  4. Shape it — pin priorities to hold them, edit estimates or dates (✎ on any row), or drag bars on the timeline. Every change recomputes the whole schedule.
  5. Freeze it — save the agreed order as a baseline. It stores the schedule and the inputs that produced it, so later re-plans are measured against it.
  6. Compare — the live order against a baseline, or two baselines against each other. The gap between "what we agreed" and "what we now propose" is the decision.

Assumptions & levers (⚙)

These shape the schedule. Each one changes what the order looks like — so change them with intent.

LeverWhat it means
period startweek 0 — every target date is measured from here. Without it, dates cannot become weeks and the verdicts stay empty.
WIP modelhow many initiatives may be in flight across the org. strict: every initiative counts. drum-gated: only work touching the drum counts. off: no org limit. The Order view prices all three side by side.
org WIP limitthe number of initiatives in flight at once. Blank derives it from the drum pod's track count.
estimate modeleffort: the estimate is total work, divided across the team's lanes — 60 pair-weeks on a 3-pair team takes ~20 weeks. wall-clock: the estimate is one lane's duration.
bufferprotective time added to each chain (25% by default) so a slip eats the buffer instead of the promise. 0 commits on the raw finish — no safety net.
full-kit gateminimum readiness (all inputs present) before an initiative may start
per-pod limitinitiatives at one pod at once; blank is uncapped
starts per quarterhow much change the org can absorb per quarter
splitting taxthe overhead (in weeks) of dividing one piece of work across a team's lanes. Blank turns splitting off.
chunk sizework above this many weeks divides across lanes; no track carries more. 45 weeks chunks as 20+20+5 at a 20-week cap.
drum target utilizationholds releases so drum load stays under the ceiling, keeping headroom for escapes
calendar windowsfreezes, site holidays and reduced-capacity spans — drawn on the timeline and enforced by the scheduler

The Order view

This is where the schedule lives: every initiative ranked, with the dates you can promise and the reason each start is where it is.

Reading it

  • The verdict banner is the whole answer in one sentence: how many dates are at risk, and the worst one by name.
  • Your stated priority order is the working plan. The engine's suggestion appears as ↳N beside the rank. ⚡ Optimize offers its best order — accepting is always your call.
  • Binds names what set each start: dependency, pod capacity, WIP limit, freeze, kit gate, drum stagger, or a pin you placed.
  • Expand a row (▸) to see the per-pod slices: start, finish, latest safe start and slack per pod.

Fever chart (Order view)

Each dot is a dated initiative read at its target date. The x-axis is how much of the chain should be done by then; the y-axis is how much buffer the date consumes. Under the green line is comfortable, amber is burning fast, red has spent it. The zones match the Observe fever chart, so both views read the same way.

Timeline

The order drawn as spans: one row per initiative, or one block per pod with a lane per track. The same schedule, seen from two angles.

Reading it

  • solid bar — scheduled work. lighter tail — the buffer. ◆ — the target date.
  • → — waits on another pod.
  • idle lanes — capacity the plan leaves free, shown on purpose: a pod that looks idle is a pod you can load.
  • hover a week number — the calendar date. hover a bar — start, finish, slack, and its lane ladder if split.

Every drag is a round-trip through the scheduler, not a redraw. The bar lands where the constraints allow — occasionally it snaps to a legal week next to your drop, and if a drop would overlap other work it is refused with the conflict named. After a drag, check the chip: if it says inputs have moved, your agreement no longer describes this plan.

Per-pod capacity loss

The card header shows a pod's own loss when it has one. An ops-heavy pod at 30% converts every track into 70% of a week of product work — its bars are honestly longer. Set it in Plan setup ▸ Sites & capacity.

Baselines

A baseline freezes the current order with the inputs that produced it — roster, estimates, parameters. It is how you say "this is what we agreed", and the measuring stick for every later change: drift, re-plans, and actuals are all measured against it.

Click the chip in the header (any view) to open the baselines drawer. It holds everything:

  • Save current order — type a name (25 characters is the limit — names are labels), save. The schedule and its inputs are frozen. The chip turns green with the name and a matches tag.
  • make active — switches which baseline the plan is measured against. Only one is active at a time; it is the one the chip names.
  • Delete — removes a mistaken save. Two clicks, on purpose. If the deleted one was active, the newest remaining baseline becomes active.

The chip is the live status: matches means the plan still agrees with the baseline; inputs have moved means the roster or estimates changed since the freeze — the drawer then highlights the save row, because freezing the changed plan as a new baseline (keeping the old one) is almost always the move. Nothing is renamed: baselines are immutable snapshots.

Comparing

  • compare — the live order against the selected baseline: rank, start and commit deltas per initiative, additions and removals.
  • vs… — two baselines against each other: what changed between the agreed order and a later proposal.

The health report

⎙ Report (beside the view tabs) opens one printable card — the thing to read before a planning meeting, and the thing to bring to it.

  • the headline — "20 of 29 initiatives will not finish inside the period" is the plan's honest sentence. An all-clear plan says so.
  • verdicts — the distribution, with the problem initiatives named and their lateness.
  • capacity — pods at or over the edge, with ρ and their track count; the constraint pod is marked drum.
  • date conflicts — date-locked initiatives contending for the same pod: these cannot both have their dates until one moves.
  • top remedies — what the engine would change, priced by its effect on the whole portfolio, with how many other initiatives move. Each row's full options opens the priced panel in the Order view.

Print gives you exactly the card, black on white, one page. Every number on it comes from the same schedule the views render — the card cannot disagree with the Order view.

Sites & timezones

Cross-team handoffs are not equal: work passing between Dublin and Warsaw shares almost a full day; work passing between Warsaw and Denver shares nothing. Plan setup ▸ Sites is where you tell Conway where your teams actually are, so those seams are priced differently.

  • Every site your roster references is listed automatically, with its timezone not set to start.
  • Set a site's IANA timezone (Dublin → Europe/Dublin) and its working hours (default 09:00–17:00 local).
  • Once two sites are set, the handoff between their pods is priced from the hours their working days actually share on the modelled date — daylight saving included.

Until a site's timezone is set, handoffs touching it use the pessimistic default — the plan will look worse than reality for exactly those pairs, and the editor leaves them visible so you know what to fill. The game's coordination costs use the same table.

Use the banner on the timeline (or ⚙ Assumptions on the Order view) to choose; the schedule recomputes immediately, and the dialog shows what each model costs for your plan. Choosing is not cosmetic: strict allows only a few concurrent initiatives, off allows all — pick what your org actually runs.

The theory under all of it: every system has one constraint at a time. Protect it, feed it full-kit work, and let everything else wait — the dates you protect are the dates you keep. The ? chips throughout the app carry these definitions in context.
